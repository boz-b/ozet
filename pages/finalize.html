The tiny number is not a financial problem

The latest capacity test stopped because cash and accumulated fees differed by roughly 10−24 dollars, far below one cent. Separate rounding of 37,500 charges explains the drift. The program used 34 significant digits and an excessively strict agreement check. This is numerical bookkeeping noise, not evidence of lost capital.

That stopping threshold has no useful economic justification here. Keeping extra digits internally can prevent rounding error. Requiring an entire study to stop over this residual does not improve the investment answer.

What changes at basis-point precision?

1 basis point (bp) = 0.01% = $100 on $1 million. It is a relative measure, not a universal number of decimal places.

Use financial precision, with a fixed stopping point

Proposed rule: show returns to 1 bp; retain extra internal digits, price ticks and share rules. Check accounting drift against a tight dollar limit, such as one cent per account, with an independent full-run reconciliation. Never grant a fresh error allowance on every charge.

Use 1 bp as a proposed result comparison threshold for ending wealth relative to starting capital and maximum drawdown. Check changed trades and pass/fail decisions separately. Blanket 1 bp constraint relaxation is untested; unchanged results cannot be promised. Missing data, borrowing rules and the untouched holdout remain separate constraints.

Is this realistically reproducible on this machine?

The present workflow has not demonstrated that. The latest invented capacity attempt ran about two hours before being stopped, with roughly 9.1 GB of summed process memory and no memory guard firing. An earlier real attempt stopped on memory pressure. A full successful duration remains unknown.

Relaxing a tolerance removes false failures; it does not reduce simulation counts or automatically speed up arithmetic. The share of runtime due to 34-digit arithmetic is unmeasured.
