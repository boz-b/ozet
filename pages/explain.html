01 / PURPOSE

The question behind VHF

A stock represents ownership in a company. An investor buys it hoping that future cash distributions and the price at which it can be sold will compensate for the money committed and the risk taken. A portfolio is the collection of those investments, cash and any other rights or obligations.

Picking an attractive stock is only one part of investing. You must also decide how much to buy, which other investments to hold, when to change the portfolio, and how to respond when facts or prices change. A strategy specifies that entire decision process. A quantitative strategy expresses enough of it as rules that the same inputs can produce the same decision again.

VHF began by studying books on stock selection, portfolio risk, trading systems and research discipline. Their ideas become hypotheses to investigate. Software then makes the assumptions precise and records their consequences. The first substantial historical application is a stock-selection method called quality momentum: buy relatively strong stocks while considering how their gains developed.

01IdeaWhy might an opportunity exist?
02SignalWhat observable rule identifies it?
03PortfolioWhat should we own, and how much?
04ExecutionWhat could we actually buy or sell?
05EvidenceWhat did we earn, risk and learn?

Each step needs the previous one, but also adds a new question. A good signal can lead to a badly diversified portfolio. A sensible portfolio can be expensive to trade. A profitable-looking simulation can rely on information nobody had at the time. VHF's purpose is to make those differences visible.

Two kinds of work, two kinds of truth

The research library explains what a book or paper argues. The engineering record explains what VHF actually implemented and checked. A passing software test does not prove an investment theory. A missing payment record does not refute momentum. Keeping these two records separate makes each more useful.

02 / CAPITAL

Follow the money before following a signal

A return measures the change in the value of an investment relative to what you started with. An investment worth $100 that becomes $110 has returned 10%. If it pays a distribution, that payment is also part of the economic result; a price chart alone may omit it.

Return = (ending value + distributions − starting value) / starting value

This simple formula assumes one holding with no external deposits or withdrawals. Ending value excludes distributions counted separately in the formula, so each dollar is counted once.

For a portfolio, the contribution of an investment also depends on its weight: the fraction of capital allocated to it. A 10% stock gain is not a 10% portfolio gain if most of the portfolio is elsewhere.

Illustration: $100,000 of capital, one 2% stock position, no fees or distributions
ItemAt purchaseAfter a 10% stock gain
Shares held20 shares × $10020 shares × $110
Stock value$2,000$2,200
Uninvested cash$98,000$98,000
Total portfolio value$100,000$100,200
Portfolio return—$200 / $100,000 = 0.20%

Net asset value, or NAV, is the value of holdings plus cash and valued claims, less liabilities. A claim is a right to receive something later—for example, a declared dividend or the cash owed after a merger. That right may have value today without being spendable cash today. If its value cannot be established, the full portfolio's NAV can be unknown.

Returns also compound. Growing $100 to $120 and then losing 10% leaves $108. You gained 8% over the whole period, and suffered a 10% drawdown from the $120 peak. Final return and the journey taken to reach it answer different questions.

Capital
The money committed to the strategy.
Position
The actual number of shares or other units held.
Target weight
The share of portfolio value the strategy would like to allocate.
Profit and loss
The dollar gain or loss, often abbreviated P&L.
Drawdown
The decline from a previous peak in portfolio value.
03 / OBSERVATIONS

A decision can only use information it had

Before asking which stock looks best, we need to know what a historical investor could have observed. A December financial statement might be published in February. A company's ticker can change. A business that later failed must still appear in an earlier investment universe if it was eligible then.

Point-in-time data means preserving the information available at each decision date, including revisions and publication delays. Using today's surviving companies to reconstruct yesterday's choices creates survivorship bias. Using a later revision or tomorrow's price creates look-ahead bias. Either can make a strategy appear better informed than it was.

When did it happen?

Economic date

The date a price was traded or a financial period ended.

When could we act on it?

Availability date

The time the necessary information reached the decision process. It can be later than the economic date.

VHF's historical development study uses a current snapshot of older data and a modeled delay between a month-end observation and the next opening trade. It verifies which input files were used and restricts their dates. Those controls do not establish that every historical revision and delivery time is known. The study is explicitly a current-vintage reconstruction.

The same stock can need two price representations

A two-for-one split replaces one old share with two new shares. A $100 share might become two $50 shares without creating or destroying wealth. A raw price chart would show a 50% drop; that would be a false loss for the holder.

Illustration: a two-for-one split, with no intervening market movement
MeasurementBefore the splitAfter the split
Raw price per share$100$50

Comparable example trading volume1,000 old shares2,000 new shares
Example dollars traded$100 × 1,000 = $100,000$50 × 2,000 = $100,000

A coherent total-return series accounts for splits and distributions when measuring an investment's economic return. Raw prices and raw share units are needed to simulate orders, fills and cash movements. Dollar trading volume must multiply a price and share quantity expressed in compatible units. The volumes above are chosen to illustrate that identity; actual market volume need not double after a split.

Method: Donadio and Ghosh on data and trading systems, and Paleologo on research data and validation. VHF's specific vendor conversion follows the Sharadar adjustment documentation; its September correction is explained in the development record.

04 / THE FIRST SIGNAL

Find relative winners

Cross-sectional momentum compares different stocks at the same date and ranks them by past return. The hypothesis is that relative strength can persist for a time. Possible explanations include investors responding gradually to information and institutions being slow to change positions. These are research explanations, not a guarantee that the next winner will keep winning.

Momentum does not mean finding the fastest-growing business. Nor does this particular strategy ask whether an index is above its own moving average. It asks: among the stocks we could consider today, which have performed relatively well? A relative winner can still have a negative return when most stocks are falling.

VHF starts with a declared universe: the stocks eligible for consideration. Its current monthly rule uses supported US-dollar domestic common stocks, sufficient price history, a size screen and a liquidity screen. Market capitalization is the market value of a company's equity. Average daily dollar volume, or ADV, measures the average dollar amount traded each day.

Current VHF development rules; precise choices made by this project

A percentile locates a value within a group: roughly 40% of reference capitalizations fall at or below the 40th-percentile threshold. The size reference is formed before liquidity filtering. Its membership matters because removing a stock can change the threshold for other stocks. This is also why the newer ex-TECO variation excludes that company before any thresholds or rankings, consistently across the compared portfolios.

Look back, then skip the latest month

The current primary signal takes thirteen month-end levels and compares the first with the penultimate. This measures eleven included monthly returns, leaving the latest month out of the momentum calculation. The skip-month convention separates intermediate-term momentum from short-term price reversal effects.

If the relevant total-return level rose from 100 to 125, momentum is 25%. Once all eligible stocks have a score, VHF can rank them. A high rank is an input to the next step; it is not yet an instruction to buy.

Research lineage: Gray and Vogel, Quantitative Momentum, chapters 5–7. The Kenneth French Data Library illustrates the prior 2–12 month convention; its portfolio construction is different from VHF's fifty-stock implementation.

05 / THE PATH

The same gain can tell two different stories

Imagine two stocks that both rise from 100 to 120. One advances gradually; the other spends most of the period flat and then jumps. Their endpoint returns are identical. Their paths suggest different information and attention patterns.

In this project, quality describes the return path. It does not mean good management, a strong balance sheet or high accounting profitability. Gray and Vogel's discussion of continuous information motivates examining whether strength came through many ordinary moves or a few attention-grabbing events.

Measure 1 / FIP

How continuous was the path?

Frog-in-the-pan, or FIP, compares positive and negative return days and accounts for the sign of overall momentum. Lower values are preferred. For a positive-momentum stock, many more up days than down days give a lower value.

VHF uses 252 daily returns ending before the skipped month. Zero-return days remain in the denominator.

Measure 2 / MAX

Was there a large upward jump?

MAX is the largest signed daily return in the skipped month. Lower values are preferred. It measures a particular form of lottery-like upward movement.

For daily returns of +1%, +2% and −8%, MAX is +2%, not 8%. The daily challenger uses a different absolute-jump measure.

How the current primary selects fifty stocks

  1. Rank eligible stocks by momentum. Keep the highest 10%, rounding the count up.
  2. Rank path quality inside that shortlist. Give equal importance to the percentile ranks of negative FIP and negative MAX, so lower underlying values score better. These percentile ranks describe each stock's relative position within the shortlist.
  3. Select the fifty strongest quality scores. Assign each a target weight of 2%. If the shortlist contains fewer than fifty stocks, mark the formation unavailable.

For example, 1,000 eligible stocks produce a shortlist of 100, then fifty targets. The method does not quietly broaden the universe or select a smaller portfolio just because its rules cannot form fifty names. Ties are broken consistently using permanent security identifiers.

Optional detail · the FIP formula and an invented example
FIP = sign(momentum) × (negative days − positive days) / 252

A positive-momentum stock with 150 up days, 90 down days and 12 flat days has FIP = (90 − 150) / 252 ≈ −0.238. This counts directions, not the size of each move. MAX adds a separate jump-related observation. Neither statistic fully describes a return distribution.

Research: Da, Gurun and Warachka, Frog in the Pan; Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw, Maxing Out; Gray and Vogel's path-quality discussion. The exact windows, 50/50 percentile combination and fifty-name rule above describe VHF's declared implementation.

06 / CONSTRUCTION

A ranking becomes a set of commitments

A signal orders candidates. Portfolio construction turns that ordering into desired weights, subject to rules about concentration, risk and funding. The primary long-only strategy targets fifty stocks at 2% each. Long-only means buying and owning stocks, rather than betting against them through borrowed shares.

Those are target weights at a decision. Prices subsequently move, so actual weights drift. A rebalance proposes trades to change the existing portfolio toward a new target. It must consider what is already owned, the cash available and the ability to sell old holdings—not just produce another list of attractive names.

The daily challenger uses a declared 70/20/10 mix of momentum, log-price trend fit and a low-absolute-jump measure, under a 252/21 trading-session convention. It is not the same FIP/MAX recipe as the primary. Keeping it separate lets the comparison test a genuine alternative instead of changing the primary after looking at outcomes.

Shorting means borrowing shares, selling them, and later buying shares back to return them. Falling prices can benefit the short position; rising prices create losses. Borrow availability and fees matter. Positive and negative targets in a spreadsheet do not establish that borrowing and trading those shares would have been possible.

Time is part of the strategy

VHF has ten registered trial constructions: monthly primary and challenger, each with long-only and factor-neutral forms, plus three calendar variations of the primary, also in both forms. The calendar variations are quarterly, overlapping quarterly and seasonality-aware.

An overlapping quarterly portfolio keeps several independently funded groups, or cohorts, alive at the same time. A maturing group recycles its own capital. Their weights and wealth then drift. Averaging three return series, or resetting each group to one-third every month, would describe different economics.

Portfolio and sizing principles: Advanced Portfolio Management. Momentum calendar motivation: Quantitative Momentum. The ten-trial construction family and challenger coefficients are VHF engineering choices; their development is documented on the next page.

07 / RISK

Fifty stocks can still share one big bet

A portfolio can hold many companies and still be concentrated in a common exposure. Technology shares may respond together to interest rates. Small companies may be sensitive to credit conditions. Most long-only equities carry substantial exposure to broad stock-market moves.

A factor is a shared driver used to describe these movements. A stock's beta is its estimated sensitivity to a factor. For example, market beta of 1.2 suggests a tendency to move about 1.2% when the market moves 1%, within the model. Individual news and estimation error can dominate on any particular day.

Stock return ≈ market contribution + other common-factor contributions + stock-specific return

Correlation describes how closely returns move together. Covariance combines that relationship with the sizes of their fluctuations. A portfolio risk model uses these relationships to estimate how much total value might vary. Counting names alone cannot provide that estimate.

Illustration: two assets, each with 20% annual volatility, held at equal weights
Relationship assumedPortfolio volatilityInterpretation
Perfect positive correlation20.0%They move together; the second name adds no diversification in this model.
Zero correlationAbout 14.1%Some fluctuations offset; portfolio risk is lower.

Volatility measures the variability of returns. It treats upside and downside variation alike; it does not capture every risk, including an inability to sell, an unpaid claim or a sudden change in correlations. The table assumes stable relationships purely to illustrate the calculation.

Dollar neutrality is not factor neutrality

A portfolio that is 50% long and 50% short has zero net dollar exposure and 100% gross exposure, the sum of absolute weights. But if its longs have market beta 1.4 and its shorts beta 0.6, its market exposure is 0.5 × 1.4 − 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.4. Equal dollars have not removed market sensitivity.

VHF's factor-neutral construction adjusts a proposed long/short portfolio to satisfy named market, sector and size constraints while staying close to its initial weights. A constraint is a limit or requirement the solution must meet. If no permitted solution exists, the target is unavailable. Even an estimated neutral target can develop risk as prices, exposures and holdings change.

Optional detail · what an optimizer is solving

An optimizer searches for weights that balance an objective with constraints. VHF's neutral construction minimizes changes from its initial target while enforcing specified exposure and weight requirements. The broader library also studies the trade-off between expected return, covariance risk and transaction costs. Those are related tools, not a claim that every historical long-only result used a full optimized risk allocation.

Sources: Paleologo, Advanced Portfolio Management and The Elements of Quantitative Investing; the Quantopian lectures reinforce covariance estimation, factor diagnostics and uncertainty.

08 / OWNERSHIP

An intended trade is not a completed trade

A target says what the portfolio wants. An order requests a transaction. A fill records a completed transaction with a quantity, price and costs. Only completed fills and actual corporate actions change the units owned.

VHF's historical adapter forms decisions using the allowed earlier observations, sizes whole-share orders, and then applies opening checks. The next opening price can make the order unaffordable or breach a concentration limit. Trading volume can be inadequate. An old position may be impossible to sell. In those cases, a selected portfolio may never become the held portfolio.

Small trading costs can compound into a large hurdle

Turnover measures trading relative to capital. Spread is the gap between buying and selling quotes. Market impact is the change in execution price associated with trading. Fees add another charge. A strategy that changes many positions must earn enough before costs to absorb all of them.

Corporate actions add a second clock

A company can split shares, pay a dividend, merge into another company or stop trading. These are corporate actions. An economic entitlement and its later payment or share delivery can occur on different dates. A ledger must distinguish them.

Illustration: a cash acquisition, with dates established separately by evidence

This is why a missing final stock quote cannot simply become an assumed cash payment. A later cash receipt cannot be treated as already spendable at an earlier opening. The claim and uncertainty must remain visible until the relevant evidence is established.

09 / COMPARISON

“It went up” does not establish an edge

If a stock portfolio gains 10% while a broad comparable opportunity set gains 15%, the result means something different from gaining 10% while that opportunity set loses 5%. A benchmark provides a comparison chosen to answer a specific question.

Comparators use the same decision axis and declared equity universe
ComparatorHow it is formedQuestion it asks
Raw momentumTop fifty stocks by momentum, without the primary's path-quality overlay.Does the quality step add value beyond selecting winners?
Eligible equal weightEqual weight in every eligible stock.Does selection add value beyond broad eligible ownership?
Eligible cap weightWeights proportional to capitalization within the eligible universe.How does the result compare with larger companies receiving more weight?
BILAn external short-term Treasury-bill fund price series.What did a cash-like alternative do over the same dates?

“Raw momentum” means the signal has no path-quality overlay. It does not mean using unadjusted prices for returns. Likewise, BIL is an external comparison, not the return automatically credited to idle cash. VHF's current ledger assumptions give uninvested cash zero interest.

There are several useful summaries. Cumulative return measures total growth. Volatility measures fluctuation. Maximum drawdown measures the worst decline from a previous peak. A Sharpe ratio compares average excess return with its variability. Each compresses a different aspect of the path; none resolves missing observations or incorrect accounting.

A fair comparison must retain failures

If a stock was selected but later cannot be valued, dropping it and scaling the survivors back to 100% changes the strategy after seeing the outcome. VHF keeps the original allocation and reports unavailable exact results where necessary. A separate zero-recovery scenario can show one assumption's consequence; it does not turn that assumption into an observed settlement.

Evaluation and attribution: The Elements of Quantitative Investing and the Quantopian lectures. The benchmark family above is the project's declared development comparison.

10 / RESEARCH DISCIPLINE

Test without learning the answer first

A backtest applies a rule to historical observations. It is useful because it exposes the rule to many market conditions. It is dangerous because the researcher already has access to the outcomes and can keep adjusting the rule until the history looks attractive.

Training data helps estimate or choose a rule. A later test period asks how that fixed choice behaved afterwards. Walk-forward evaluation repeats this sequence: use an earlier window, assess a later window, then advance through time. The split must be based on information timing, not merely row positions.

Evaluation sequence / Schematic
01Earlier dataEstimate the rule
02Boundary controlsRemove overlapping labels
03Later testApply the fixed choice
04AdvanceRepeat in time order
05Final holdoutKeep unread until authorized

A future-return label can overlap a later test window even when its starting observation lies in training. Purging and separation gaps address that timing problem.

Purging removes training examples whose outcome periods overlap a protected boundary. An embargo adds separation around periods that might share information. These controls are particularly important when a position is held for several months or multiple cohorts overlap.

There is another problem: try enough variants and one can look good by chance. This is multiple testing. Changing a lookback, a rebalance month, a filter or a universe can each spend research evidence. VHF records declared trials and later amendments, including the retrospective ex-TECO variation. A previously inspected period does not become untouched again when the code is corrected.

A holdout is a reserved period kept separate from development. VHF's final historical holdout remains unread. Complete strategy-family inference and final holdout evaluation have not been completed. An isolated attractive chart therefore cannot identify a validated winner.

11 / THE PRESENT SCOPE

A broad research ambition, one concrete development frontier

The library reaches beyond momentum. Paleologo provides the portfolio and risk framework. Donadio and Ghosh explain trading-system mechanics. Bennett develops volatility, options and correlation research. Quantopian contributes practical statistical discipline. These sources help VHF consider how strategies might complement one another and share data, risk and execution infrastructure.

For example, a value strategy compares prices with measures of business value; it asks a different selection question from momentum. A volatility strategy may compare the movement priced into options with the movement that later occurs. Those ideas can require different instruments, information and accounting. Their presence in the library is not evidence that VHF has run or validated them historically.

The variant consistently excludes it before formation and preserves the original records. Its code and focused checks are complete; its actual historical performance has not yet been calculated.
